Ionic bonding 📤📥

Happens between a metal and a nonmetal, atoms from opposite sides of the periodic table.

  • Metals have 1, 2 or 3 electrons in their outer shell. Easiest fix: give them away. Then the full shell underneath becomes the outer one.
  • Nonmetals have 5, 6 or 7 outer electrons. Easiest fix: grab a few more to reach 8.

The NaCl story (table salt) 🧂

  1. Sodium (Na) has one lonely outer electron (3s¹). Chlorine (Cl) needs one more (3p⁵ → 3p⁶).
  2. Na gives its electron to Cl. Now both have full shells and are stable.
  3. But Na lost a negative charge → it's now Na⁺ (a cation). Cl gained one → Cl⁻ (an anion).
  4. Opposite charges attract like magnets 🧲. That attraction is the ionic bond. (The official name: Coulombic attraction.)

Another example: MgO

Mg: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²  → gives 2 e⁻ → Mg²⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (like neon [Ne])
O: 1s² 2s² 2p⁴  → takes 2 e⁻ → O²⁻: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (also like neon [Ne])

Both end up with neon's perfect configuration. This works because their electronegativities are very different: one strongly wants to give, the other strongly wants to take.

🔑 Ionic bonding needs (these are the blanks in the slides)
• It requires electron transfer.
• It needs a large difference in electronegativity.
• It is the predominant bonding in ceramics.
Examples from the slides: NaCl, MgO, CaF₂, CsCl.

Features of ionic materials

  • 💪 Strong, high-energy bonds → high melting point
  • 🧭 Non-directional: a + ion attracts − ions equally in every direction
  • 🔨 Hard and brittle: they crack (cleave) instead of bending
  • 🔌 Insulators: electrons are locked onto ions and can't move. Often transparent.
3

Covalent bonding 🤝

Happens between nonmetals. They have 4 or more outer electrons. Giving away that many would cost too much energy, and so would grabbing that many. So instead they share.

🤝 Picture it Two kids each have half the money for a game. Neither can buy it alone, so they share one copy: it spends time at both houses. In a covalent bond, each atom puts in one electron, and the shared pair spends time around both atoms, holding them together.

Features of covalent materials

  1. Often hard
  2. Inorganic covalent compounds usually have high melting points
  3. Can be very strong (diamond 💎, the hardest natural material)
  4. Can be weak (bismuth, Bi)
  5. Directional: the shared pair sits between two specific atoms, so bonds point in fixed directions (unlike ionic or metallic)
4

Metallic bonding 🌊

Metal atoms hold their outer (valence) electrons only loosely. In a solid metal, every atom lets go of them, and they form a shared "sea" or "gas" of electrons flowing around all the atoms.

The bond = the attraction between the positive metal ions and the negative electron sea around them. The sea is the glue.

Features of metallic materials

  • ⚡🔥 Great conductors of electricity and heat: the free electrons carry it
  • ✨ Opaque and shiny
  • 🪝 Relatively ductile: atoms can slide over each other and the sea keeps holding them
  • 📦 High packing density (you'll see why in Lecture 4)
  • Generally weaker than ionic and covalent bonds
  • Always crystalline

Bonding forces & energies

💃 Picture this Two atoms are like two dancers holding hands with a spring between them. Drift too far apart and the hands pull them back (attraction). Get too close and they bump (their electron clouds, both negative, push each other away: repulsion). There's one perfect distance where pull and push cancel. That distance is r₀.

The two forces

  • Attractive force FA: depends on the type of bond (ionic, covalent, metallic). The "pull".
  • Repulsive force FR: from the two negative electron clouds overlapping. The "push". Only matters when atoms are really close.
FN = FA + FR

The net force FN is just the total. At the equilibrium spacing r₀ they cancel exactly:

at r = r₀ :   FA + FR = 0

For many atoms, r₀ ≈ 0.3 nm.

From force to energy

Energy and force describe the same thing in two ways. The slides write E = ∫F dr, which means the force is the slope of the energy curve. Energies add the same way:

EN = EA + ER
🏞 Think of a valley Plot energy against distance and you get a valley shape. A ball dropped anywhere on it rolls to the bottom and stays there. The bottom is where the atoms "want" to be:
• Its position (left–right) is r₀, the equilibrium spacing.
• Its depth is E₀, the bonding energy: the energy needed to pull the two atoms completely apart.
• At the bottom the ground is flat → slope = 0 → force = 0. The same fact as FA + FR = 0, seen from the energy side!
• The slides' blank: the most stable state is where the energy is lowest.

🎮 Play with it: drag the slider to move the ions

Real numbers for a K⁺–Cl⁻ pair: A = 1.436 eV·nm, B = 5.86×10⁻⁶ eV·nm⁹, n = 9. Orange = attraction energy EA, blue = repulsion energy ER, green = their sum EN.

📝 Example 1: Find r₀ and E₀ (the big one)

We're given the net energy (Eq. 2.17), the sum of EA = −A/r (Eq. 2.9) and ER = B/rⁿ (Eq. 2.11):

EN = −Ar + Brn

The negative part is attraction, the positive part is repulsion. A, B and n are just constants for the material. Treat them like ordinary numbers.

  1. Rewrite with negative powers (Tool 1):
    EN = −A·r−1 + B·r−n
  2. (a) Differentiate term by term (Tool 2: bring the power down, subtract 1):
    dENdr = A·r−2 − nB·r−n−1 = Ar2 − nBrn+1 = FN
  3. Set it equal to zero (bottom of the valley), calling that special r "r₀":
    Ar₀2 = nBr₀n+1
  4. Collect the r₀'s on one side: multiply both sides by r₀², divide by nB (Tool 3):
    AnB = r₀2r₀n+1 = r₀2−(n+1) = r₀1−n
  5. (b) Undo the power by raising both sides to 1/(1−n):
    r₀ = (AnB)1/(1−n)
  6. (c) Plug r₀ back into EN to get the depth of the valley:
    E₀ = −A(A/nB)1/(1−n) + B(A/nB)n/(1−n)
    Done! 🎯
🎁 Bonus: a cleaner way to write E₀ From step 3, B/r₀n = A/(n·r₀). Substitute that in and it simplifies to
E₀ = −Ar₀(1 − 1n)
For K⁺–Cl⁻: r₀ = (1.436 / (9 × 5.86×10⁻⁶))1/(1−9) ≈ 0.279 nm and E₀ ≈ −(1.436/0.279)(8/9) ≈ −4.57 eV. Check it with the slider above: the bottom of the green valley sits right there.

What the energy curve tells an engineer

The shape of the valley predicts real properties. This is the end of Lecture 3:

🌡 Melting temperature Tm

A deeper valley (larger E₀) means the atoms are harder to separate.
Larger E₀ → higher Tm.
That's exactly what Example 2 showed.

📏 Thermal expansion αℓ

Heat makes atoms vibrate. The valley is lopsided (steep on the left, gentle on the right), so vibrating atoms drift outward → the material grows.
Deep, narrow valley → small αℓ (barely expands).
Smaller E₀ → larger αℓ.
Thermal expansion:  ΔLL₀ = αℓ (T₂ − T₁)

ΔL = change in length, L₀ = original length, T₂ − T₁ = temperature change.

For polymers, the weak secondary bonds between chains control most physical properties (remember the spaghetti 🍝).

★

Summary

If you remember only these points, you're in great shape:
  • Atoms bond to get a full outer shell: give/take (ionic), share (covalent) or pool into an electron sea (metallic).
  • Ionic (metal + nonmetal, large electronegativity difference): strong, non-directional, hard, brittle, insulating, high Tm. Typical of ceramics. E.g. NaCl, MgO.
  • Covalent (nonmetals share pairs): directional, often hard; can be very strong (diamond) or weak (bismuth).
  • Metallic (positive ions in an electron gas): good conductors, opaque, ductile, densely packed, crystalline.
  • FN = FA + FR, EN = EA + ER. At r₀: FN = 0 and EN is at its minimum, E₀.
  • Section 2: EN = −A/r + B/rⁿ → set dEN/dr = 0 → r₀ = (A/nB)1/(1−n) → E₀ = −(A/r₀)(1 − 1/n).
  • Section 2: plotting bonding energy vs melting point gives roughly a straight line; copper (1085 °C) ≈ 345 kJ/mol.
  • Larger E₀ → higher melting point, smaller thermal expansion. Ceramics: large E₀; metals: variable; polymers: weak bonds, low Tm.

Check yourself

Na + Cl: which bond type, and who gives the electron?
Ionic. Sodium (metal) gives one electron to chlorine (nonmetal).
Which bond type is directional?
Covalent: the shared pair sits between two specific atoms.
Why do metals conduct electricity?
Their valence electrons form a free-moving "sea" that can carry current.
At r₀, what is the net force between the two atoms?
Zero. Attraction and repulsion cancel, which is also the bottom of the energy valley where the slope is zero.
What does E₀ physically mean?
The bonding energy: the energy needed to pull the two atoms completely apart. Deeper valley = stronger bond.
Why do we set dEN/dr = 0?
The minimum of a curve is where its slope is zero, and the minimum of the energy curve is the stable equilibrium position.
Material X has bonding energy 600 kJ/mol. Higher or lower melting point than aluminum?
Higher. Use the slider in Example 2: it lands around 2200 °C.
Ceramics barely expand when heated. Why?
They have large bonding energies, so a deep, narrow energy valley → low coefficient of thermal expansion.
🧠 One-line summary Atoms bond by giving (ionic), sharing (covalent) or pooling (metallic) electrons. Attraction and repulsion balance at r₀, and the depth of the energy valley (E₀) predicts melting point and thermal expansion.
