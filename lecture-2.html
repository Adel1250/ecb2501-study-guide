What is an atom?

An atom is the smallest piece of a substance that is still that substance. Keep cutting a gold bar in half… the last piece that is still "gold" is one gold atom.

Every atom has two regions:

  • Nucleus: the tiny centre. Made of protons and neutrons. Holds almost all the mass.
  • Electron cloud: surrounds the nucleus. Holds almost all the space.
🏟 Picture it If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a marble in the centre and the electrons would be buzzing around the stands. Atoms are mostly empty space!

The three particles

ParticleChargeWhereMass
Proton p⁺+ positivenucleusheavy (~1 amu)
Neutron n0 neutralnucleusheavy (~1 amu, about the same as a proton)
Electron e⁻− negativeshells around the nucleussuper light (a proton weighs ~1,800 electrons!)
🧠 Memory trick Proton = Positive, Neutron = Neutral, the electron is the tiny extra one flying around.

A normal (neutral) atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel out.

3

Key definitions (exam favourites!)

🔢 Atomic number, Z

Z = number of protons (= number of electrons in a neutral atom). It's the atom's ID card: change it and you have a different element. H: Z = 1, C: Z = 6, Fe: Z = 26.

⚖️ Atomic mass, A

A ≈ protons + neutrons. The mass of the nucleus (electrons are too light to count).

👯 Isotopes

Same element (same protons), different neutrons, so a different mass. Carbon-12 has 6p + 6n; carbon-14 has 6p + 8n. Twins, one carrying a heavier backpack 🎒.

📊 Atomic weight

The average mass of an element's naturally occurring isotopes. That's why carbon is 12.011, not exactly 12.

📏 Atomic mass unit (amu)

1 amu = 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. A tiny "ruler" for weighing atoms.
4

The mole (the only "math" here)

Atoms are so tiny we count them in huge packs, like eggs come in dozens:

PackHow many
1 dozen12
1 mole6.022 × 10²³ (Avogadro's number, NA)
✨ The magic link
1 amu/atom = 1 g/mol
The same number works in both units. Iron: 55.85 amu per atom, or 55.85 g per mole.
Mini-example: mass of ONE iron atom
55.85 g/mol ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ atoms/mol ≈ 9.27 × 10⁻²³ g
You'll use exactly this idea in Lecture 4 to calculate the density of a metal.

Electrons live in shells 🧅

Electrons live in layers around the nucleus called shells (energy levels), like an onion. Inner shells fill first. Each shell has sub-shells named s, p, d, f, holding 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons.

Shell1st (K)2nd (L)3rd (M)4th (N)
Max electrons281832

Reading "1s² 2s² 2p⁶": shell number, then sub-shell letter; the little number = how many electrons are in it. Add the little numbers and you get Z (neon: 2 + 2 + 6 = 10 ✅).

🎮 Build an atom: slide to add protons and electrons

🔑 The most important idea of this lecture Valence electrons = electrons in the outermost shell. Most atoms are not stable because the outer shell isn't full. Atoms with a full outer shell (helium, neon, argon, krypton: the noble gases) are happy and don't react.

Every other atom wants a full outer shell, and gets it by giving, taking or sharing electrons. That desire is why bonds exist. That's Lecture 3. 🎯
6

The periodic table: the cheat code 🗺

Elements in the same column have the same number of valence electrons, so they behave alike.

ColumnTendencyBecomes
Group 1 (Na, K)give up 1 e⁻+1 ion
Group 2 (Mg, Ca)give up 2 e⁻+2 ion
Group 16 (O, S)accept 2 e⁻−2 ion
Group 17 (F, Cl)accept 1 e⁻−1 ion
Group 18 (He, Ne, Ar)nothing, already full—

Simple rule: the further apart two elements are (left vs right), the more one wants to give and the other wants to take.

7

Secondary bonds: the weak "sticky" forces 🫧

Lecture 3 covers the strong "primary" bonds. These are the weak ones.

Van der Waals bonds

  • Weak attractions found in all materials, but most important in plastics and polymers.
  • A polymer = long chains of carbon atoms. Inside each chain the bonds are very strong (covalent). Between chains, only weak van der Waals forces hold on.
🍝 Picture spaghetti Each noodle is strong, but noodles slide past each other easily. That sliding between chains is why plastics bend and melt at low temperatures.

Hydrogen bonds

  • In water (H₂O), the oxygen end is slightly negative (δ−) and the hydrogen ends slightly positive (δ+).
  • The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of its neighbour: a hydrogen bond (drawn as a dotted line).
  • Each water molecule hydrogen-bonds to four others. 💧 That's why water boils at such a high temperature for a tiny molecule, and why ice floats!

δ means "a little bit of" charge: not a full + or −, just slightly.

★

Summary

If you remember only these points, you're in great shape:
  • Atom = tiny heavy nucleus (protons + neutrons) + light electron cloud (mostly empty space).
  • Proton +, neutron 0, electron −. A proton is ~1,800× heavier than an electron.
  • Z = number of protons. Isotopes = same protons, different neutrons. Atomic weight = average over the natural isotopes.
  • 1 amu = 1/12 of a ¹²C atom. 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms. 1 amu/atom = 1 g/mol.
  • Electrons fill shells (2, 8, 18, 32) and sub-shells s, p, d, f. Configuration like 1s² 2s² 2p⁶.
  • Valence electrons (outer shell) decide behaviour. A full outer shell = stable (noble gases).
  • Left of the table: give up electrons (+ ions, electropositive). Right: accept electrons (− ions, electronegative).
  • Secondary bonds (van der Waals, hydrogen bonds) are weak; they matter most in polymers and water.
✓

Check yourself

Which particle has no charge?
The neutron.
Carbon has Z = 6. How many protons and electrons in a neutral carbon atom?
6 protons, 6 electrons.
What are isotopes?
Same element (same protons), different number of neutrons.
Copper's atomic weight is 63.5. What's the mass of 1 mole of copper?
63.5 g.
Why is neon unreactive but sodium very reactive?
Neon's outer shell is full (2s² 2p⁶). Sodium has one extra electron (3s¹) it wants to get rid of.
Will chlorine tend to gain or lose an electron?
Gain one, becoming Cl⁻ (try Z = 17 in the atom builder!).
🧠 One-line summary An atom = heavy nucleus (protons + neutrons) + light electrons in shells. Atoms are unstable unless their outer shell is full, so they give, take or share electrons, and that's how bonds form.
