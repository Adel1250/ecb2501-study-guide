1

Materials made history 🏺⚔️

We name ancient times after materials: the Stone Age, the Bronze Age, the Iron Age. Whole civilizations are named after the best material they had!

  • The earliest humans only had natural materials: stone, wood, clay, skins.
  • Then people discovered you can change a material's properties by heating it or mixing in other substances (that's how bronze and steel were born).
  • Only recently did scientists understand why this works: properties come from the material's internal structure.

Every big technology needed a materials breakthrough first: 🚗 cars needed cheap steel; 📱 phones and computers need semiconductors.

What does "structure" mean?

"Structure" = how the inside is arranged. We can zoom in at different levels, like Google Maps zooming from a country down to a single house:

LevelSizeWhat you see
Subatomicinside one atomelectrons and the nucleus, their energies
Atomic~0.1 nmhow atoms organise into molecules or crystals
Nano< ~100 nmtiny particles made of groups of atoms
Micro100 nm → a few mmfeatures seen with a microscope 🔬 (e.g. grains)
Macroa few mm → ~1 mthings you see with your eyes 👀

1 nm = one billionth of a metre. A human hair is about 80,000 nm thick.

What is a "property"?

A property = how a material responds when you do something to it. You poke it (a stimulus), it reacts (a response). Properties don't depend on the size or shape of the piece.

#FamilyYou do this…Examples
1Mechanical 💪apply a load/forcestiffness (elastic modulus), strength, fracture resistance
2Electrical ⚡apply an electric fieldconductivity, dielectric constant
3Thermal 🔥change the temperaturethermal expansion, heat capacity
4Magnetic 🧲apply a magnetic fieldmagnetization, susceptibility
5Optical 💡shine light on itrefractive index, reflectivity
6Deteriorative 🧪expose to chemicalscorrosion resistance (rust!)
🧠 Memory trick "My Engineer Teacher Makes Old Designs": Mechanical, Electrical, Thermal, Magnetic, Optical, Deteriorative.
5

Why should you care?

Simple: everything you design is made of a material: a beam, a bridge cable, a column, a pipe. When choosing, an engineer asks three questions:

  1. What conditions will it face? (loads, temperature, weather) → tells you the properties you need.
  2. Will it get worse over time? (rust, cracks, wear)
  3. How much will it cost? 💰 Often the deciding factor. A perfect material is useless if it's too expensive.

⚠️ Wrong material = disaster: the Liberty ships

In World War II, the USA built thousands of cargo ships very fast by welding steel plates. Some of them cracked in half, a few while still in the harbour! Why?

  • 🥶 Cold made the steel brittle. Steel that bends nicely at room temperature can snap like glass in the cold (the ductile-to-brittle transition). They sailed in the freezing North Atlantic.
  • ⬛ Square hatch corners concentrated stress, and cracks started there.
  • 🔥 Welding made the hull one continuous piece, so a crack could run all the way around. (With rivets, a crack stops at the plate edge.)
  • 👷 Inexperienced welders left defects.

The fixes: better steel (less sulfur and phosphorus), rounded corners, crack-arresting straps, better welding.

🎯 Lesson Knowing how your material behaves isn't optional. It can decide whether a structure is safe.

Same material, different structure → different property

Three disks, all aluminum oxide, placed over printed text:

6

Types of materials

Crystalline vs amorphous (a sneak peek of Lecture 4)

  • Crystalline: atoms in a neat repeating pattern over long distances. All metals and alloys are crystalline: iron, steel, copper, brass, bronze, aluminum…
  • Amorphous: no regular pattern. Example: glass.

Think: a crystal is soldiers in a parade; amorphous is a crowd at a concert.

The 4 families of engineering materials

FamilyExamplesTypical behaviour
Metalssteel, aluminum, copper, titaniumstrong, stiff, ductile (bend before breaking), conduct electricity & heat, heavy
Ceramicsglass, concrete, oxides, porcelainvery stiff & hard, heat-resistant, but brittle (shatter), insulators
PolymersPVC, nylon, polyethylene, rubberlight, flexible, cheap; low stiffness and low melting point
Compositesfibreglass (GFRC), carbon fibre (CFRC), wood, reinforced concretea mix of two or more, giving the best of both

Composites live in the overlaps: metal–ceramic, metal–polymer, ceramic–polymer. 🏗 Reinforced concrete is the one you'll meet most as a construction engineer: concrete (great when squeezed) + steel bars (great when pulled).

Polymers (plastics) also split into thermoplastics (melt and reshape: acrylics, nylon, PVC), thermosets (set permanently: epoxies) and elastomers (stretchy: rubbers, silicones). Metals split into ferrous (contain iron: steels, cast iron) and non-ferrous (aluminum, copper, titanium).

Summary

If you remember only these points, you're in great shape:
  • Grades: 60% coursework (quizzes, 2 midterms, assignments) + 40% final exam.
  • Materials science studies the link between structure and properties; materials engineering uses it to design materials for a job.
  • Structure levels, from small to big: subatomic → atomic → nano → micro → macro.
  • 6 property families: mechanical, electrical, thermal, magnetic, optical, deteriorative.
  • Choosing a material: service conditions → deterioration over time → cost.
  • Liberty ships: steel turned brittle in the cold + square corners + welded hulls → ships cracked. Wrong material choice = danger.
  • Crystalline = ordered repeating atoms (all metals); amorphous = random (glass).
  • 4 families: metals (strong, ductile, conductive, dense), ceramics (stiff, hard, brittle), polymers (light, flexible, weak), composites (best of both, e.g. reinforced concrete).
