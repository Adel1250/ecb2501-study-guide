Why study crystal structures?

Last lecture: how atoms bond. This lecture: how they arrange themselves in a solid. That arrangement explains real properties:

  • 👓 The same material can be transparent when non-crystalline but opaque or translucent when crystalline (remember the aluminum-oxide disks in Lecture 1).
  • 🔩 Knowing iron's crystal structure explains what happens when steel is heat-treated to make it stronger.

Crystalline vs amorphous

🍊 The orange stall Throw oranges into a box and they land in a random jumble: that's amorphous (like glass). Stack them carefully at a fruit stall in a neat repeating pyramid: that's crystalline (like metals).
The neat stack fits more oranges in the same space (denser), and each orange is snug against its neighbours. For atoms, snug and orderly means lower energy, so metals naturally prefer to crystallize.
  • Crystalline: atoms sit in a repeating 3D pattern with long-range order. When a metal solidifies, its atoms line up in this pattern by themselves.
  • Amorphous (= non-crystalline): no long-range repeating pattern. Happens with complex structures or rapid cooling (no time to line up!).

Energy & packing (Section 3, first slide)

Non-dense, random packing

Neighbour distances are all different → a shallow, irregular energy well. Less stable.

Dense, ordered packing

Every neighbour sits at the ideal bond length → a deeper, regular energy well. More stable. ✅

The atomic hard-sphere model

To describe crystals we pretend atoms are solid balls with a fixed diameter, and neighbouring balls touch. Simple, and it works surprisingly well.

A lattice is the 3D grid of points sitting at the centres of those balls: the "skeleton" of the crystal.

The unit cell = the Lego brick 🧱

A crystal repeats in every direction. Instead of describing billions of atoms, we describe the smallest box that, copied over and over, builds the whole crystal. That box is the unit cell. Its edge length is called a (the lattice parameter).

The slides draw each cell three ways: (a) hard-sphere, with balls at real size touching; (b) reduced-sphere, with small balls so you can see inside; (c) the lattice, just the points. The viewer below does (a) and (b):

🎮 Spin the unit cells (drag to rotate)

⚠️ The #1 idea people miss: atoms are SHARED between cells An atom on a cube corner is shared by 8 cubes meeting there → only 1/8 belongs to our cell.
An atom in the middle of a face is shared by 2 cells → 1/2 is ours.
An atom fully inside → the whole 1 is ours.
(In HCP, a hexagon corner is shared by 6 cells → 1/6.)

5

The cubic structures

🧊 Simple Cubic (SC)

  • a = 2R → V = (2R)³ = 8R³
  • Atoms: 8 corners × 1/8 = 1
  • CN = 6 (left, right, front, back, up, down)
  • APF = (1 × 4/3 πR³) / 8R³ = π/6 = 0.52: almost half the box is empty! That's why almost no metal uses SC (polonium is the famous exception).

🧊 Body-Centered Cubic (BCC)

Same cube plus one atom right in the middle. The corner atoms no longer touch each other; they touch the centre atom, along the body diagonal.

The Section 3 slide does this with Pythagoras twice: face diagonal X² = a² + a² = 2a², then body diagonal a² + X² = (4R)² → 3a² = 16R² → a = 4R/√3.

  • V = (4R/√3)³ = 64R³/(3√3) ≈ 12.32 R³
  • Atoms: 8 × 1/8 + 1 centre = 2
  • CN = 8 (the centre atom touches all 8 corners)
  • APF = 0.68 (proved in Section 3 below)
  • Examples: chromium, iron (α), molybdenum, tantalum, tungsten

🧊 Face-Centered Cubic (FCC)

An atom on every corner plus one in the middle of each of the 6 faces. Atoms touch along the face diagonal.

  • Atoms: 8 × 1/8 + 6 × 1/2 = 1 + 3 = 4
  • CN = 12
  • APF = 4 × (4/3)πR³ / (16√2 R³) = 0.74, the tightest packing possible for equal balls!
  • Examples: aluminum, copper, gold, lead, nickel, platinum, silver

Section 3 problems

📝 Example 1: volume of aluminum's unit cell

Given: atomic radius of aluminum R = 0.143 nm. Find: unit cell volume in m³.

  1. Know the structure. Aluminum is FCC (the hidden fact! The question expects you to know it, or read it from the 16-metals table below). So V = 16√2 R³ ≈ 22.6 R³.
  2. Convert to metres first. 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m → R = 0.143 × 10⁻⁹ m.
  3. Cube it. (0.143 × 10⁻⁹)³ = 0.002924 × 10⁻²⁷ = 2.924 × 10⁻³⁰ m³ (cube the number and the power: (10⁻⁹)³ = 10⁻²⁷).
  4. Multiply. V = 22.6 × 2.924 × 10⁻³⁰ = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁹ m³ ✅
🤯 How small is that? About 15 quintillion (1.5 × 10¹⁹) of these unit cells fit in a single cubic millimetre.

📝 Example 2: show that APF for BCC = 0.68

  1. Atom volume: 2 atoms × (4/3)πR³ = (8/3)πR³ ≈ 8.38 R³
  2. Cell volume: a³ = (4R/√3)³ ≈ 12.32 R³
  3. Divide: APF = 8.38 R³ / 12.32 R³ = 0.68 ✅
⭐ Notice the magic R³ appears on top and bottom and cancels. APF never depends on the atom's size, only on the arrangement. SC is always 0.52, BCC always 0.68, FCC and HCP always 0.74.
⚠️ Small typo alert One Section 3 slide shows the BCC volume as 12.81R³. The correct value is (4/√3)³ R³ = 12.32R³ (the lecture's table says 12.31, which is just rounding). The APF of 0.68 only works with 12.32, so trust that.

Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP)

🍊 Back to the oranges Lay oranges flat, packed as tight as possible: each one is surrounded by 6 others in a hexagon. That's layer A. Add a second layer: the oranges sink into the dips of the first. That's layer B. Third layer goes directly above A. Repeat: A B A B… That's HCP.
(Put the third layer in a new spot instead, A B C A B C…, and you get FCC! Same 0.74 packing, different stacking.)

The HCP cell is a hexagonal prism (see it spin in the viewer):

  • Top and bottom faces: a hexagon of 6 atoms plus 1 in the centre.
  • Middle layer: 3 atoms tucked into the dips, at height c/2.
  • Two lengths: a = side of the hexagon (= 2R), c = height of the prism.

  • CN = 12 (6 in its own layer + 3 above + 3 below)
  • APF = 0.74, same as FCC
  • Cell volume = 24√2 R³ ≈ 33.9 R³ (the lecture's table prints 33.84; 33.94 is the exact value)
  • Examples: cadmium, cobalt, titanium (α), zinc, magnesium

Prove the ideal c/a = 1.633

The idea in one sentence The middle atom M sits in a dip on top of 3 bottom-layer atoms (J, K, L) that all touch each other and touch it. These 4 atoms form a little pyramid (a tetrahedron) with every edge = 2R = a. Find the pyramid's height: that height is c/2.
  1. Spot the touching atoms. J, K, L (bottom layer) and M (middle layer) all touch, so every edge of the pyramid is
    JM = JK = 2R = a
  2. Where is M? Exactly halfway up the cell, directly above point H (the centre of triangle JKL):
    MH = c/2
  3. Find JH from the base triangle. JKL is equilateral (all angles 60°). The line from J to the centre H cuts J's angle in half → 30°. The horizontal part of JH is half of JK, i.e. a/2:
    cos 30° = a/2JH  →  JH = a/2√3/2 = a√3
  4. Pythagoras in triangle JHM (right angle at H, since M is straight above H):
    JM² = JH² + MH²  →  a² = a²3 + c²4
  5. Solve for c². a² − a²/3 = 2a²/3, so
    c²4 = 2a²3  →  c² = 8a²3
  6. Square root, divide by a:
    ca = √(8/3) = 1.633 ✅
⭐ What "ideal" means 1.633 is what you get for perfect hard balls. Real metals are close: magnesium ≈ 1.62, titanium ≈ 1.59, zinc ≈ 1.86 (zinc's atoms are a bit "squashed").

Density computations ⚖️

Density = how heavy something is for its size. If we know the unit cell, we can calculate a metal's density without ever weighing it!

ρ = mass of atoms in cellvolume of cell = n AVC NA
SymbolMeaningWhere you get it
nnumber of atoms in the cellFCC = 4, BCC = 2 (sharing rule)
Aatomic weight (g/mol)periodic table (Lecture 2)
VCvolume of the unit cell (cm³)a³, using a in terms of R
NAAvogadro's number6.022 × 10²³ atoms/mol

Why it works: A/NA is the mass of one atom (Lecture 2!). Multiply by n atoms and you have the mass inside the cell. Divide by the cell's volume. Done.

📝 Lecture example: theoretical density of copper

Copper: R = 0.128 nm, FCC, A = 63.5 g/mol.

  1. FCC → n = 4, VC = 16√2 R³
  2. Units! Density is in g/cm³, so use cm: R = 0.128 nm = 1.28 × 10⁻⁸ cm
  3. VC = 16√2 × (1.28 × 10⁻⁸)³ = 22.63 × 2.097 × 10⁻²⁴ = 4.75 × 10⁻²³ cm³
  4. ρ = (4 × 63.5) / (4.75 × 10⁻²³ × 6.022 × 10²³) = 254 / 28.6 = 8.89 g/cm³
  5. Measured: 8.94 g/cm³. Super close! 🎯

Why are metals denser than ceramics and polymers?

In general: ρmetals > ρceramics > ρpolymers. These are the blanks in the slide:

  • Metals: close packing (metallic bonding) + often higher atomic masses → heaviest.
  • Ceramics: less dense packing, often lighter elements.
  • Polymers: low packing factor (often amorphous), light elements (C, H, O) → lightest.
  • Composites: intermediate values, generally lower densities.

Single crystals, polycrystals & isotropy

Single crystal

The regular repeating pattern continues without interruption through the whole piece. Rare and special: diamond single crystals (for abrasion resistance), quartz, and jet-engine turbine blades.

Polycrystalline: what most materials actually are

Most engineering materials are made of many small single crystals stuck together. Each small crystal is called a grain. Grain sizes range from about 1 nm to 2 cm.

❄️ How grains form (solidification) ① As liquid metal cools, tiny crystal "seeds" (nuclei) appear in random places, each tilted differently. ② They grow by grabbing atoms from the liquid. ③ Neighbouring crystals bump into each other and stop. They end up with irregular shapes. ④ Under a microscope you see the grains, separated by dark lines called grain boundaries (where two differently-tilted patterns meet).
Think of ice forming on a pond from many starting points at once.

Isotropic vs anisotropic

Anisotropic

The property value depends on the direction you measure.
Seen in single crystals.
Example: stiffness of BCC iron: E along the cube edge = 125 GPa, along the body diagonal = 273 GPa. Same crystal, different direction!

Isotropic

The property is the same in all directions.
Polycrystals whose grains are randomly oriented: the directions average out.
Example: ordinary polycrystalline iron: E = 210 GPa in every direction.

But if the grains are textured (e.g. a rolled or deformed metal where grains line up in a preferred direction), the polycrystal becomes anisotropic again.

X-ray diffraction: how we "see" crystals 🔦

Atoms are far too small to see with light. So we shine X-rays at the crystal. Their wavelength is about the same size as the spacing between atoms.

  • X-rays bounce (scatter) off planes of atoms.
  • If two scattered waves come out in step (in phase), they add up → a strong signal: constructive interference 🔊
  • If they come out out of step, they cancel → nothing: destructive interference 🔇
  • Strong signals only appear at special angles that depend on the spacing of the atom planes. Measure those angles → work out the crystal structure.

The machine (diffractometer): an X-ray source T, the specimen S, and a detector C, which rotate around an axis O. The output is a diffraction pattern: a graph of sharp peaks, like a fingerprint of the crystal (the slides show one for α-iron).

Uses: identifying what a material is made of (and how much), measuring residual stresses, and measuring crystal size.

★

Summary

If you remember only these points, you're in great shape:
  • Crystalline = long-range repeating order (metals, many ceramics). Amorphous = no long-range order (glass; favoured by rapid cooling). Ordered packing = lower energy.
  • Hard-sphere model: atoms are touching balls. The unit cell is the repeating brick; a = edge length.
  • Sharing rule: cube corner ⅛, face ½, inside 1 (HCP hexagon corner ⅙).
  • APF = n·(4/3)πR³ ÷ Vcell. Recipe: where do atoms touch → a in R → count atoms → divide.
  • SC: a = 2R, 1 atom, CN 6, APF 0.52. BCC: a = 4R/√3, 2 atoms, CN 8, APF 0.68. FCC: a = 2√2R, 4 atoms, CN 12, APF 0.74. HCP: 6 atoms, CN 12, APF 0.74.
  • Section 3: Al (FCC, R = 0.143 nm) → V = 22.6R³ = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁹ m³; BCC APF = 8.38R³/12.32R³ = 0.68.
  • Section 4: tetrahedron JKLM with edges a, height c/2, JH = a/√3 → a² = a²/3 + c²/4 → c/a = √(8/3) = 1.633.
  • Density: ρ = nA / (VCNA). Copper → 8.89 g/cm³ (measured 8.94). ρmetals > ρceramics > ρpolymers.
  • Polycrystals = many grains with grain boundaries. Anisotropic = direction-dependent (single crystals); isotropic = same in all directions (randomly oriented grains).
  • X-ray diffraction: constructive interference from atom planes at special angles reveals crystal structure (and residual stress and crystal size).

Check yourself

How many atoms belong to one FCC unit cell, and why?
4. Corners: 8 × 1/8 = 1. Faces: 6 × 1/2 = 3. Total 4.
In BCC, where do atoms touch, and what does that give you?
Along the body diagonal: 4R = a√3, so a = 4R/√3.
Which packs denser: BCC or FCC?
FCC (0.74 vs 0.68).
Why is the APF the same for gold and aluminum, though their atoms differ in size?
Both are FCC, and R³ cancels in the APF formula. Only the arrangement matters.
How many atoms are in one HCP cell?
6. 12 × 1/6 + 2 × 1/2 + 3 = 2 + 1 + 3.
In the HCP proof, why is MH = c/2 and not c?
The middle-layer atom M sits halfway between the bottom and top faces.
HCP and FCC both have APF 0.74. What's different?
The stacking order: HCP is ABAB…, FCC is ABCABC….
What 4 things do you need to calculate a metal's density?
n (atoms per cell), A (atomic weight), VC (cell volume from R), and NA. Then ρ = nA / (VCNA).
Is a single crystal isotropic or anisotropic?
Anisotropic: properties depend on direction.
🧠 One-line summary Metals crystallize into SC, BCC, FCC or HCP cells. Find where atoms touch → a in terms of R → count shared atoms → APF and density. Real metals are polycrystals, and X-rays reveal their structure.
